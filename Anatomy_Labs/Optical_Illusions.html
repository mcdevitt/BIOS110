Anatomical Locus: Retinal Ganglion Cells (Center-Surround)

Why Your Eye Does This: Lateral Inhibition

Your retina does not send raw pixel brightness to the brain. Instead, retinal ganglion cells have circular center-surround receptive fields: light hitting the center excites the neuron (+), while light hitting the surrounding ring inhibits it (−). This edge-sharpening circuit is called lateral inhibition.

Why do the dots vanish when you look straight at them? Your central fovea has tiny, high-resolution receptive fields that fit completely inside a single white street, avoiding the surrounding black corners.

Anatomical Locus: S, M, L Cones & Opponent Ganglion Channels

Why Your Eye Does This: Cone Fatigue

Human color vision relies on three cone photoreceptor types—S (blue), M (green), and L (red)—wired into opponent channels: Red vs. Green and Blue vs. Yellow.

Opponent-Process Afterimage Predictions
Inducing Disc Color Fatigued Cones Illusory Afterimage
Lilac (Magenta) L (Red) + S (Blue) Emerald Green (M-cone rebound)
Cyan M (Green) + S (Blue) Warm Coral-Red (L-cone rebound)

Where does the green dot come from? When each lilac disc briefly turns off to reveal neutral gray (#BFBFBF), the fatigued L (red) and S (blue) cones under-fire compared to fresh M (green) cones—sending a burst of "bright green!" signal to your brain.

Why Your Eye Does This: Brightness Constancy

In the real world, lighting changes constantly as clouds pass or shadows fall across objects. If your brain behaved like a camera light meter, a white piece of paper in the shade would look like black coal.

Physical Pixel Values vs. Cortical Perception
Tile Actual RGB Visual Context Perceived Shade
Square A RGB(120, 120, 120) Full light, bright neighbors Dark charcoal tile
Square B RGB(120, 120, 120) In cylinder shadow, dark neighbors Light off-white tile
Your brain’s unconscious calculation:
1. Square B sits inside a soft shadow cast by the green cylinder and is surrounded by dark neighbors → cortex boosts its perceived brightness.
2. Square A sits in full light surrounded by bright tiles → cortex discounts the bright light and perceives it as dark.

Anatomical Locus: Optic Disc & Primary Visual Cortex (V1)

Why Your Eye Does This: Filtering Static Noise

1. Why Troxler’s Fading Happens: Neurons in your visual system are built to detect change, not constant stimulation. When an image stays perfectly motionless on your peripheral retina (like soft, blurred clouds), sensory neurons stop firing—and the brain fills the area with the surrounding gray background.

2. Why You Don’t See Your Own Retinal Blood Vessels: Blood vessels sit in front of your photoreceptors, casting constant shadows! Because they move with your eyeball, your brain adapts them out—unless a doctor shines a moving penlight from the side during an eye exam.

3. Cortical Interpolation Across the Blind Spot: Where the optic nerve exits your eye, there are zero rods or cones. When the striped background is enabled, V1 neurons spanning the edges of your blind spot infer continuity and paint a continuous stripe right across the gap!

Anatomical Locus: V1 Orientation-Selective Simple Cells

Why Your Eye Does This: Irradiation & Edge Tilt

In the Café Wall Illusion (discovered on a tiled café wall in Bristol, UK), every horizontal mortar line is 100% straight and parallel. However, bright white tiles appear slightly larger to the eye than dark black tiles (the irradiation illusion).

Where a white tile sits opposite a black tile across a mid-gray mortar line, V1 orientation-selective neurons link the corners diagonally, creating tiny local wedges that make the entire row appear sloped!

Try the Mortar Slider: Slide the mortar brightness to pure black (0) or pure white (255). Because the mortar now merges with one set of tiles, the diagonal corner contrast disappears and the lines snap back to level!

Anatomical Locus: Visual Area MT / V5 Motion Detectors

Why Your Eye Does This: Opponent Motion Fatigue

In cortical area MT (V5), specialized neurons respond selectively to specific directions of motion—for example, inward contraction versus outward expansion—and mutually inhibit each other in balanced pairs.

When you stare at continuous inward motion for 10–15 seconds, the inward-selective neurons become fatigued and reduce their baseline firing rate. The instant the spiral stops, the rested outward-selective neurons fire stronger than the fatigued inward ones—causing a completely still pattern to appear to expand!

What about Static Peripheral Drift? Switch to Static Peripheral Drift Rings and scan your eyes around the image. Nothing on screen is moving! Asymmetric luminance steps (Black → Blue → White → Yellow) trigger tiny timing delays in motion neurons during rapid eye movements (saccades).
