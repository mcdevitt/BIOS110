Where does DNA Replication fit in the Central Dogma (DNA → RNA → Protein)?
DNA replication is the self-perpetuating horizontal loop at the start of the framework (DNA → DNA). Before a cell divides (during S phase of interphase), it must duplicate its entire genome with extreme fidelity so each daughter cell inherits a complete copy of the genetic blueprint required to run DNA → RNA → Protein.

Study Guide Sample Q1 Proof (Upper-Right Template End):
In the upper-right quadrant, the new Leading strand (blue) is synthesized continuously toward the right-moving replication fork. Because DNA Polymerase III can ONLY synthesize new DNA in the 5' → 3' direction, the growing tip at the right fork is the 3' end of the new strand. By antiparallel base pairing, the parental template strand at the right fork MUST be its 5' end!
Study Guide Sample Q2 Proof (Okazaki Fragment Order: C → B → A):
In the upper-left quadrant, the fork moves leftward while the lagging strand must grow 5' → 3' rightward (away from the fork). When the bubble first opened at the Origin, fragment C was synthesized first. As the fork opened further left, B was synthesized next, and A (closest to the moving fork) was synthesized most recently!

Mechanistic Reasoning

Why Helicase Alone Isn't Enough & Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Chromosomes

1. Helicase (Unzips H-Bonds)

Uses ATP hydrolysis to break hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs at the replication fork, separating the parental double helix into two single-stranded templates.

2. Single-Strand Binding Proteins (SSBPs)

Coat the separated single DNA strands to prevent them from spontaneously re-annealing (snapping back together) or forming hairpin loops before DNA Polymerase III can read them.

3. Topoisomerase (Relieves Supercoiling)

Unwinding a twisted helix creates overwound tension (positive supercoiling) ahead of the fork. Topoisomerase transiently nicks, swivels, and rejoins DNA strands to prevent snapping.

Feature Prokaryotes (e.g., E. coli) Eukaryotes (e.g., Humans)
Chromosome Shape & Number Single circular chromosome (no free ends) Multiple linear chromosomes (2 free telomeric ends per chromosome)
Origins of Replication 1 single origin (oriC); two forks travel bidirectionally around the circle Hundreds to thousands of origins per chromosome so huge genomes replicate rapidly during S phase
Chromatin Packaging Nucleoid-associated proteins (no true histones/nucleosomes); very fast (~1,000 nt/sec) Tightly wrapped around histone octamers (nucleosomes); chromatin remodeling must temporarily unwrap nucleosomes ahead of the fork (~50 nt/sec)
Termination & Telomeres Two forks meet on opposite side of circle; no telomeres needed Multiple bubbles fuse; terminal lagging strand suffers the end-replication problem, requiring telomerase

Discussion Sheet Q2 Verification:
New Strand:      5' ------------------------------------------ 3'
Template Strand: 3' ------------------------------------------ 5'
Which end of the new DNA will be shorter? Why?
The 5' end of the newly synthesized strand will be shorter! At the extreme 5' end of the new lagging strand, Primase places an RNA primer opposite the 3' end of the template strand. When DNA Polymerase I removes that terminal RNA primer, there is no upstream DNA strand with an exposed 3'-OH group for DNA Polymerase III or I to extend from. Thus, the 5' gap cannot be filled, leaving a 3' single-stranded overhang on the template and a shorter 5' end on the new strand.

Leading Strand vs. Lagging Strand Comparison Matrix

Both strands are synthesized by DNA Polymerase III in the 5' → 3' direction, yet their orientation relative to the replication fork creates distinct enzymatic workflows.

Property Leading Strand Lagging Strand
Template Strand Orientation (toward fork) 3' → 5' toward the replication fork 5' → 3' toward the replication fork
Direction of New DNA Synthesis 5' → 3' (toward the opening fork) 5' → 3' (away from the opening fork)
Continuity Continuous single strand Discontinuous short segments (Okazaki fragments: ~1,000–2,000 nt in bacteria; ~100–200 nt in eukaryotes)
RNA Primers Needed (Primase) Only 1 RNA primer at the origin of replication Multiple RNA primers (one at the 5' start of every Okazaki fragment)

DNA Polymerase I & DNA Ligase DNA Pol I removes the single origin primer; Ligase seals the final nick when forks meet Heavily required: DNA Pol I removes every RNA primer and replaces it with DNA; DNA Ligase forms phosphodiester bonds sealing nicks between Okazaki fragments
